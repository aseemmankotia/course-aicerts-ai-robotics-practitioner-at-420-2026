## Chapter 11: AI Ethics, Safety, and Policy in Robotics — Flashcards

| # | Front (Question) | Back (Answer) |
|---|-----------------|---------------|
| 1 | ISO/TS 15066 — what does it define? | Collaborative robot safety requirements: Power and Force Limiting (PFL), Speed and Separation Monitoring (SSM), hand-guiding, and simultaneous motion. Exam tip: it extends ISO 10218, it does not replace it. |
| 2 | ISO 10218 vs ISO/TS 15066 | ISO 10218 = general industrial robot safety (Parts 1 & 2, manufacturer + integrator requirements). ISO/TS 15066 = collaborative-specific extension (force/speed limits for shared human-robot workspace). |
| 3 | IEC 60204-1 Stop Categories: 0, 1, 2 | Category 0 = immediate power removal (uncontrolled). Category 1 = controlled stop, then power removed. Category 2 = controlled stop, power maintained. Exam tip: E-stops are typically Cat 0 or Cat 1, never Cat 2. |
| 4 | ISO 13849-1 Performance Levels (PL) | Range PLa (lowest) to PLe (highest). PLe required for severe/irreversible injury risk with high exposure and low avoidance probability. Exam tip: higher PL = higher required reliability of the safety function. |
| 5 | IEC 61508 Safety Integrity Levels (SIL) | SIL 1 (lowest) to SIL 4 (highest, e.g., nuclear/rail). Robotic safety-critical perception/stop functions typically target SIL 2–3. Exam tip: SIL 4 is NOT the default for industrial robotics. |
| 6 | Human-in-the-loop (HITL) vs Human-on-the-loop (HOTL) vs Human-out-of-the-loop | HITL: human approves each action before execution. HOTL: robot acts autonomously, human monitors/can intervene. Out-of-the-loop: no human oversight during operation. Exam tip: higher autonomy risk = stronger case for HITL. |
| 7 | Liability allocation: Manufacturer vs Integrator vs Operator | Manufacturer: liable for design/build defects in original product. Integrator: liable for defects introduced during system integration/modification (e.g., disabling safety interlocks). Operator: liable for misuse, inadequate maintenance, or ignoring warnings. |
| 8 | Bias in perception models — safety consequence example | A detection model trained on limited lighting/demographic data may fail to reliably detect certain objects, body types, or skin tones, creating uneven safety-zone protection. Exam tip: bias here is a PHYSICAL safety issue, not just a fairness issue. |
| 9 | EU Machinery Regulation 2023/1230 | Replaces the earlier EU Machinery Directive; explicitly addresses AI-enabled safety components in machinery/robots, requiring risk assessment covering evolving/learning behavior. Vendor-neutral exam note: know it exists and its scope, not exact clause numbers. |
| 10 | EU AI Act — robotics relevance | Classifies AI-driven safety components of machinery/robots as high-risk AI systems, triggering obligations like risk management, human oversight, and conformity assessment. Exam tip: focus on the high-risk classification concept, not exact compliance dates. |
| 11 | Speed and Separation Monitoring (SSM) vs Power and Force Limiting (PFL) | SSM: robot maintains a dynamic minimum separation distance from humans, slowing/stopping as distance decreases. PFL: robot allows direct contact but limits force/pressure to safe biomechanical thresholds. Both are ISO/TS 15066 collaborative methods. |
| 12 | Responsible deployment framework — key pillars | Transparency (explainable decisions), human oversight (HITL/HOTL), accountability (clear liability chain), and safety validation (testing edge cases, including bias in perception) before autonomous physical deployment. |
