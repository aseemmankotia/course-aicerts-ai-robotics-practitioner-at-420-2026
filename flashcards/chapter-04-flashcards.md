## Chapter 4: Deep Learning for Robotics — Flashcards

| # | Front (Question) | Back (Answer) |
|---|-----------------|---------------|
| 1 | Single-stage vs two-stage object detectors — key trade-off? | Single-stage (e.g., YOLO-style): one forward pass, lower latency, slightly lower accuracy — preferred for real-time edge robotics. Two-stage (e.g., Faster R-CNN style): region proposal + classification, higher accuracy, higher latency/compute. Exam tip: edge/real-time scenario → single-stage. |
| 2 | What does Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS) do in object detection? | Removes duplicate overlapping bounding boxes for the same object by keeping the highest-confidence box and suppressing others above an IoU overlap threshold. Exam tip: multiple overlapping boxes on one object → tune/apply NMS. |
| 3 | Semantic segmentation vs object detection — when to use which? | Object detection → bounding boxes around discrete objects (what + where, coarse). Semantic segmentation → per-pixel class labels (e.g., floor vs obstacle), needed for free-space/drivable-area navigation. Exam tip: 'drivable area' or 'free space' scenario → segmentation. |
| 4 | Classical PID control vs neural network control — core difference? | PID: fixed mathematical formula (P, I, D terms on error), deterministic, interpretable, stable guarantees. Neural control: learned state→action mapping from data, handles nonlinear behavior but lacks formal stability guarantees. Exam tip: safety-critical, deterministic, high-frequency loop → PID/classical control, not neural. |
| 5 | Why fuse camera + LiDAR + IMU instead of using one sensor alone? | Camera: rich semantic info, fails in low light/glare. LiDAR: accurate range, illumination-independent, lower update rate. IMU: very high-rate motion data, drifts over time. Fusion compensates for each sensor's individual weakness. Exam tip: 'camera fails in low light' scenario → rely more on LiDAR+IMU. |
| 6 | What is the standard filter architecture for fusing high-rate IMU with lower-rate LiDAR/camera pose estimates? | A Kalman-family filter (e.g., Extended Kalman Filter, EKF): IMU integration predicts pose at high rate between updates; LiDAR/camera measurements correct accumulated drift at their slower rate. This predict-correct loop is the canonical sensor fusion pattern. |
| 7 | What is transfer learning in the context of robot vision, and when is it used? | Start from a model pretrained on a large general dataset; freeze early (generic feature) layers and fine-tune later layers/head on a small robot-specific labeled dataset. Used when labeled data is scarce (e.g., a few hundred images of a new part). Exam tip: 'limited labeled data' scenario → transfer learning, not training from scratch. |
| 8 | Why does training a CNN from scratch on a small dataset (e.g., a few hundred images) usually fail for robot vision tasks? | Severe overfitting — the model memorizes the small dataset instead of learning generalizable features. Transfer learning (reusing pretrained low-level features) is the standard fix for small-data regimes. |
| 9 | Why can't deep learning perception models typically be used for a robot's low-level joint/motor control loop? | Neural network inference has higher and less predictable latency/jitter than a simple control law, and lacks formal stability guarantees. Low-level joint control (often running at high frequency, e.g., ~1 kHz) requires deterministic, provably stable control such as PID, LQR, or MPC. Neural models are better suited to the slower perception/planning layers. |
| 10 | What are the three PID control terms and what does each respond to? | P (Proportional): responds to current error magnitude. I (Integral): responds to accumulated past error, eliminates steady-state offset. D (Derivative): responds to rate of change of error, dampens overshoot. Together: output = Kp·e + Ki·∫e dt + Kd·de/dt. |
| 11 | Camera vs LiDAR vs IMU — what does each primarily contribute to perception? | Camera: semantic/visual info (object class, color, texture), fails in low light. LiDAR: precise 3D range/distance, illumination-independent, no color/texture. IMU: high-rate linear acceleration + angular velocity for short-term motion estimation, drifts without correction. |
| 12 | Why does edge/embedded compute matter when choosing a perception model for a robot? | Robots typically run on constrained, low-power, thermally-limited compute (no datacenter-class GPU). This favors lightweight single-stage detectors, quantized/pruned models, and smaller segmentation backbones over large, high-accuracy-but-slow architectures, to keep inference latency low enough for real-time obstacle avoidance and control. |
